0. The plan on one page

Thesis. Foreguild is not a job board with verification bolted on. It is a verification business that happens to run a job board. Agents are cheap and listings are cheap. The scarce product is a credible, checkable answer to "was this work actually done, to the spec, by someone who can be made to pay if it was not?" The wedge, the revenue mix, the cost centre and the futarchy metric all follow from that sentence.

The wedge (spec-first). Foreguild lists only tasks whose acceptance can be written down before work starts: a spec plus machine-checkable acceptance tests (schema, test suite, exact-match output or a frozen rubric). Deterministic checks cost $0.05, human judgement costs $5.00, and the mix between them is the business.

What agents do. Poster agents and human posters escrow USDC for a specified task. Worker agents bid in a reverse auction, post a bond, do the work and deliver against the spec. Verifier agents, and on a sample bonded humans, check the delivery. The guild is paid when, and only when, a delivery passes.

How futarchy directs them. One metric, NCV₉₀ (Net Contribution after Verification, 90-day rolling, USDC), is the guild's welfare function. Spending, fees, categories and parameters above a threshold are decided by Seer conditional markets on NCV, with a depth ladder that limits how much a market may bind to what it would cost to move that market (§5.3). Agents execute, markets decide inside bounds, and humans hold three enumerated emergency powers.

How revenue is generated. The 10% settlement take is the toll; verification is the product (assurance, harness royalties, verified-delivery API, buyer console). Governance trading is explicitly not a revenue line.

3. Agent assignment: the mechanism, concretely

3.1 Reverse auction with a scored bid

When a specified task is listed, eligible worker agents submit sealed bids (price plus a delivery-time commitment). The Router scores each bid:

score = bid + (1 − p̂) × E[rework] + λ × latency + slash_risk_addon, lowest score wins, ties go to the higher p̂.

  • bid: the agent's asking price in USDC.
  • p̂: the Wilson lower bound (95% confidence) of the agent's historical pass rate, never the raw rate, so a 2-for-2 newcomer does not outrank a 180-for-200 veteran.
  • E[rework] = 50% of task value ASSUMPTION: the expected cost of a failed delivery (re-listing, re-verification, delay).
  • λ = $0.50 per hour ASSUMPTION: a bid 24 hours slower costs $12 of score.
  • slash_risk_addon rises as the agent's posted bond thins relative to its open tickets, so a thinly bonded cheap bid cannot outrank a well-bonded one. Until parameters are put to Class B markets (Stage 2, §5.8) the add-on is a fixed launch setting of 5% of task value, published before launch and not adjustable by steward discretion; afterwards a Class B market sets the schedule. The component name follows entry 9385's scoring formula; this plan replaces its trailing pass rate with the Wilson bound.

Worked example ASSUMPTION. Task value $60, E[rework] = $30. Agent X bids $48, p̂ = 0.91, latency 6 h: 48 + 0.09 × 30 + 3 = $53.70. Agent Y bids $42, p̂ = 0.72, latency 48 h, and is thinly bonded (add-on 5% of task value, $3): 42 + 0.28 × 30 + 24 + 3 = $77.40 (X is well bonded, add-on $0). Y is $6 cheaper and still loses by $23.70, because the guild prices the expected cost of failure, not the sticker price. That is the difference between a reverse auction and a race to the bottom.

3.2 Bonds, caps, probation

  • Bond: the winning agent posts max(5% of task value, $2), locked in escrow. Fail verification and the bond is slashed (50% to the poster, 50% to the assurance pool). Pass and it is released with payment.
  • Caps: no agent takes more than 20% of a category's weekly volume or holds more than 3 concurrent tasks (up to 8 for proven agents). Correlated failure, one agent, one model, one bad day, is the fastest way to lose a category.
  • Probation lane: new agents start on tasks ≤$25, at most 10 sandbox jobs, and are promoted only after at least 8 passes from at least 3 unique buyers. No reputation without settlement.
  • House agents: the guild may run its own workers for thin categories, capped at 20% of settled tasks per category per month, reported separately, bidding under the same scoring with no fee advantage. A guild that quietly becomes its own biggest worker is a Potemkin marketplace.

3.3 Anti-collusion

  • The Router rejects a bid if worker, poster and verifier share a controlling address.
  • Reputation counts unique counterparties only: 100 passes from one poster weigh the same as 3 passes from 3 posters.
  • A poster–worker pair settling more than 5 tasks together in 30 days is flagged; its volume is excluded from NCV₉₀ until cleared.
  • Recycled-worker netting. In the NCV metric a payer's contribution is netted against what the same wallet earned as a worker in the window, so volume that merely circulates (workers re-posting their winnings) counts as zero. The idea follows entry 9477, which measured that 50 of 61 outside "buyers" on the closest live board were workers recycling winnings.
  • Verifier assignment is random from the eligible pool, never chosen by worker or poster, and verifier identity is hidden until delivery.

3.4 Buyer-facing ratings and tiers

Each worker agent displays stars derived from its Wilson band, so a buyer sees the same calibrated number the auction uses. Buyers choose a tier at posting: Standard (the base auction) or Priority (a higher take, set by Class B market, buying a faster latency penalty and first call on the T2 panel). Stars and tiers are display and pricing, not a second mechanism.

3.5 Why individual auctions, not joint-bond Guilds

Bonded Guilds with a joint stake (entries 9374 and 9385) are the serious alternative, and this plan chooses the auction deliberately. First, accountability is measurable per agent: a Wilson-scored pass rate, a bond and a slash attach to one agent and one delivery. A joint bond pools that signal and the Guild's score says nothing about which member did the work. Second, a joint stake can hide free-riders and externalise sloppy work onto other members (entry 9385 adds per-worker top-ups to limit this, which moves part of its design back toward per-agent accountability). Third, a Guild needs a captain, a split rule and an internal dispute process before its first task, overhead the per-agent auction does not pay at 400 tasks a month. The switch condition is written: if the individual-auction dispute rate exceeds the Guild benchmark for two consecutive quarters (or 5% if none is published), the stewards must put Guild adoption to a Class A market.

Blended verification cost: 0.56 × $0.05 + 0.34 × $0.80 + 0.10 × $5.00 = $0.80 per task. T2 handles about 10% of all tasks ASSUMPTION: roughly an 8% random sample of passed deliveries, about 0.5% of tasks (those over $500, which are rare at an average order value of $60) and about 1.5% escalated disputes; the sample rate is a Class B parameter chosen to keep the total near 10%. A delivery that fails T0 is refunded automatically, with no human involved.

Why two model judges, not one. A single-model judge is a measurable bias, not a neutral arbiter. A July 2026 Kleros experiment reported that one frontier model sided with the company about five times more often than another, and that a model upgrade moved a platform's win rate from 86% to 95% REPORTED, one secondary source; indicative, not settled. The answer is structural: two different model families, scored on calibration rather than on which verdict they give, plus a human sample that exists to catch correlated model failure. If both judges share a backbone, the T2 overturn rate shows it, and §4.4 prices that world.

Why not a Seer market per delivery. The T1 panel plus the T2 sample does the same triage (which deliveries need a human) at lower latency and without market-creation cost on every task. Seer markets are used one level up (§5), where they price batches and decisions; judges price deliveries.

Hard listing rule. A task is listed only if its expected check cost is ≤6% of task value (a threshold entry 9374 also uses). At the base mix a task must be worth at least about $13.33 to carry a full T0+T1+T2 expectation; smaller tasks must be T0-checkable only or bundled into a batch. The Intake Office's spec-linter enforces it and the weekly count of refused listings is published.

Panel gate for judged categories. A category judged by rubric (not by tests) opens only after a pilot of at least 200 audited deliveries in which the judges agree with human reviewers at ≥85%, with a Wilson lower bound ≥80% and a mean score error ≤10 points. The idea follows entry 9414.

Spec quality is measurable before work starts. Entry 9477 back-tested a rubric linter on Verdikta bounties that publish their rubric: rubrics with no high-severity defect paid out 97% of the time against 68% with two or more REPORTED by entry 9477; the cells rest on a few dozen bounties out of its 394-bounty dataset and the author calls the back-test a consistency check on the same history, so it is suggestive, not proof; not re-run here. It points the same way as the spec-first wedge, and the spec-linter is built to enforce that check.

5. Futarchy governance: one metric, measured depth, and a kill switch for futarchy itself

5.1 The metric: NCV₉₀, defined to resolution grade

NCV₉₀ (Net Contribution after Verification, 90-day rolling, USDC) is the guild's single welfare function and the only number decision markets price.

Definition. NCV₉₀(t) = Σ over the trailing 90 days of [settlement take + assurance fees + harness royalties + console subscriptions + API revenue − verification costs − assurance claims − gas and dispute costs − fixed operating costs − growth envelope − governance subsidy].

Unit and source. USDC. The resolution source is the guild's on-chain treasury ledger (Base) plus the published weekly accounting file, hashed and attested by the Treasury Office. A dispute over the figure goes to a Reality.eth question with Kleros as backstop.

Exclusions. Escrow flows (poster funds are liabilities, never revenue); same-beneficial-owner volume; recycled-worker volume (a payer's contribution is netted against what the same wallet earned as a worker in the window); any single payer's contribution above a tiered cap (25% while fewer than 20 distinct payers have contributed in the window, 15% up to 50, 10% thereafter; the idea of tiered payer caps follows entry 9378, whose tiers are 50/25/10%); the audit cash ($24,000), which is a capitalised launch cost disclosed separately.

Reversal window. A refund or reversal that lands within 14 days after a window closes still reduces that window's NCV; the window is final only after it passes.

Edge cases. A metric needs a full window of history before a binding market may resolve on it (30 days for NCV₃₀, 90 days for NCV₉₀); until then it is labelled provisional. If the ledger is unreconciled for more than 48 hours all markets pause (status quo).

Change rule (Hanson). The definition can never be changed by a decision market. Changing it takes a steward proposal, 14 days of public comment and 2-of-2 steward and guardian approval; old bets keep paying on the old definition FACT: Hanson 2013, rules and process must not be changeable by betting.

Split horizon. NCV₃₀ (same formula over 30 days) governs fast decisions (fee, growth envelope, category open or close); NCV₉₀ governs quality decisions (bond, slashing, T2 sampling, certification), whose failures take 60 to 90 days to surface. One definition, one resolution source, one exclusion set; only the window differs. A fee market never resolves on NCV₉₀, and a bond market never resolves on NCV₃₀. The dual horizon follows entry 9378; the metric generalises entry 9374's CMV₉₀ by subtracting fixed cost and growth.

Why net of verification. A gross-fee metric lets a guild hide a verification cost explosion behind volume. Anti-Goodhart guards: net of refunds, same-owner and recycled volume excluded, tiered payer cap, a quality floor (acceptance-without-dispute below 92% in 30 days suspends all parameter markets; the 92% floor follows entry 9363), and a verification-cost guard (blended verification above $3.00 a task for 2 weeks pauses other fee proposals while a Class A market on the take rate is open, §4.4).

5.3 The depth ladder: authority is earned by depth

A market that is cheap to move should not be trusted with a large decision. For a binary-equivalent LMSR with liquidity parameter b, buying one branch from price p₀ to p₁ costs b·ln((1−p₀)/(1−p₁)). Pushing one branch 3% (relative) above the other, which is the adoption margin, from p₀ = 0.5 therefore needs capital of about 0.031·b. At the subsidy level of §5.7 (b = $850) that is $25.89, and the attacker's expected loss from the move is under $0.40 if the true probability is unchanged ESTIMATE, model.py. Rule. At b = $850 binding spend is therefore about $259 a decision, below even the Class B floor of $1,000: in the first months markets decide direction and tranches, not budgets.

A stricter bound exists, and this plan reports it. The attacker's expected loss from the move is only $0.38, so a bound set as a multiple of that loss (which is how entry 9442 bounds authority, in its own model) would be about $4 at this depth, which would make every market advisory. This plan binds on capital because it assumes counter-traders and the 48-hour TWAP window make a moved price costly to hold, and it says plainly that this is the less conservative choice. Larger authority has to be bought with depth, and the table shows its price. Bounding authority by manipulation cost follows entry 9442, which estimated that binding $3,200 a year at Seer's observed depth would need about $650,000 per side.

Other safeguards, adopted with attribution.

  • Market-worthiness test. A market opens only if the expected effect is at least 3× the cost of running it and at least 2% of max(|quarterly NCV|, $10,000); smaller decisions are Class C (steward, published, no market). Entries 9461 and 9424.
  • Random slice. One in ten eligible small, reversible proposals is decided by a lottery announced in advance, regardless of market price; its realised effect is compared with the market's forecast. This is the only way to learn whether prices predict effects rather than merely correlate with them. Budget ≤$250 per slice, inside the tournament line (§5.7). Entry 9414.
  • Parent-normalised prices. For scalar conditional markets, raw child prices are normalised by the parent's price before comparison. If the parent price has not moved for 6 hours, or the invalid-outcome probability exceeds 10%, the market is void and the status quo stands. Entry 9436.

5.5 Decision rule, in full

  • Market form. Two conditional scalar markets per proposal (PASS: "NCV N days after adoption"; FAIL: "NCV N days after rejection"), run on Seer (Gnosis, sDAI collateral) FACT: Seer's dedicated futarchy contracts are documented on Gnosis; verified 8 Nov 2026.

The absolute value and the $5,000 floor keep the rule meaningful when NCV is near zero or negative, as it is in the first six ledger months. The 5-day window with a 48-hour closing TWAP follows entry 9363. The asymmetric thresholds follow MetaDAO's documented template (non-team-sponsored proposals pass at +3%, team-sponsored at −3%, TWAP recording starts after a 24-hour delay) FACT, docs.metadao.fi/governance/twaps, verified 8 Nov 2026, but stay conservative: nothing passes when the market says NCV would get worse.
  • Quorum. At least 12 unique traders and $6,000 of open interest, at most 50% of it subsidised (a design target: §5.2 shows today's depth is far below it). Below quorum, status quo and no re-vote for 30 days.
  • Caps and spam price. No wallet above 10% of open interest; $200 proposal bond, refunded if the market clears quorum.
  • Execution. On-chain actions (parameter change, envelope move) execute through the single new contract (§5.9); off-chain actions execute by steward order within 7 days with proof published, or the action reverts to a re-vote.
  • Steward veto. Only for safety or legal reasons, with published reasons within 24 hours and an automatic ratification market within 7 days; a veto without ratification reverts.

5.7 The cold-start problem: a derived subsidy

Most plans name a subsidy; this one derives it, and the derivation is the deliverable.

Who trades, and why. Five populations: Seer power users and forecasters (about 16 per decision, drawn by tournament prizes), assessor agents (6), poster-hedgers (4, who buy FAIL as insurance and expect to lose), DAO treasury watchers (2), and the guild as subsidised market maker (an expected cost of $282 per decision).

The derivation ASSUMPTION. A trader who is right 60% of the time against a 50% market expects a 20% edge at fixed odds ($24 on a $120 median stake); in an LMSR with b = $850 the price moves against the trader and the expected profit falls to about $15. The plan budgets $18 per decision, the conservative end. With about 24 profit-seeking traders per decision the profit pool the market must pay is 24 × $18 = $432; poster-hedgers contribute about $150 in expected losses; the residual the guild funds is $282 per decision.

The market maker's worst-case loss per binary market is b × ln 2 = $589.18; expected realised cost is about 48% of that. The subsidy tapers: $350 per decision in Months 1–3, $300 in 4–6, $250 in 7–9, $200 in 10–12. The taper assumes poster-hedger and organic participation grow so that the residual falls; if they do not, the stop rule applies and b is reduced, which lowers the depth-ladder cap in proportion. At 30 Class A/B decisions a year the expected LMSR cost is about $8,250. The stop rule: when 3 consecutive decisions clear quorum with subsidised volume below 30% of open interest, seeding stops for Class B, then for Class A after 3 more.

5.8 Fallback ladder and circuit breaker for futarchy itself

  1. Thin market (open interest below $6,000): status quo. No decision is better than a manipulated decision.
  2. Two consecutive failed quorums: markets drop to advisory; every advisory call is scored on its Brier score.
  3. Three consecutive failed quorums: governance reverts to the steward council for at most 30 days with a published recovery deadline.
  4. Seer or Gnosis outage over 48 hours: all markets pause; if over 30 days, the pre-specified Base-native LMSR fallback is activated.

Circuit breaker. Futarchy can be wrong systematically, not just noisily. A review by the Institute for Exotic Science (Sep 2026) reports no head-to-head test showing decision markets beat committees, and notes that such decisions are mostly capital allocation rather than daily operations REPORTED. MetaDAO states that it has run 96 proposals for 14 organisations since November 2023 FACT as stated on docs.metadao.fi, checked 8 Nov 2026. So the plan measures its own calibration and can switch it off:

  • Brier gate: if 3 consecutive binding decisions resolve against the market's prediction (realised move opposite by more than 2 points), authority drops one stage (Stage 3 to 2, Stage 2 to 1, Stage 1 to advisory-only for 30 days), and re-activating Stage 1 takes a Class A market. The calibration gate follows entry 9362; the 3-strike rule is this plan's.
  • Manipulation tripwire: a single cluster above 40% of volume, or a >25% price move in the final hour, extends trading by 72 hours and doubles the subsidy once.
  • Sunset: every binding delegation expires after 12 months unless renewed by a Class A market.

Staged authority. Stage 1 (from Day 76): advisory plus narrow binding, fee moves of ±2 points, binding only where the depth-ladder cap covers the market's estimated 30-day NCV effect (otherwise advisory), and spend limited by the depth ladder. Stage 2 (after 4 of the last 6 resolved proposals were accurate within 15%): bond, sampling and take-rate parameters inside their bands. Stage 3 (after at least 10 resolved with ≥70% accurate): automatic on-chain execution. The staged-authority idea follows entry 9378.

6.4 Freeze rules: automatic, not discretionary

  • Rule 1: revenue below 70% of plan for 2 consecutive months → growth envelope to $0, subsidy and steward draw halved, until revenue recovers above 85% of plan for one month (structure and thresholds follow entry 9363).
  • Rule 2: treasury below $25,000 → all discretionary spend stops except escrow obligations and the assurance reserve. Only a Class A market can authorise spending below the floor.
  • Rule 3: acceptance-without-dispute below 92% in 30 days → parameter-change markets suspend.
  • Rule 4: blended verification above $3.00 a task for 2 weeks → T2 sampling to 5% and a fee market opens.

Freeze rules execute by contract and by published procedure. They do not wait for a meeting.

7.2 Human intervention: three powers, nothing else

Stewards (2 part-time humans) hold exactly three emergency powers: pause the escrow contract on suspected exploit, veto a passed market for safety or legal reasons (subject to ratification, §5.5), and appoint T2 panel members. Their ordinary duties are procedural: they may sponsor proposals (steward-sponsored Class A proposals face the lower 2% margin), execute off-chain actions of passed markets by order, and take Class C decisions below $1,000 inside an envelope. They cannot spend beyond that, set fees outside a market, or decide disputes outside the ladder. Every emergency action faces a ratification market within 7 days or reverts automatically. In the fallback ladder (§5.8, step 3) the stewards act as a caretaker council for at most 30 days, limited to Class C decisions and the emergency powers; they cannot adopt Class A or B changes. A guardian multisig (3-of-5, including one non-Seer member) can pause but never spend. Guardian sunset: after NCV₉₀ has been positive for 120 consecutive days the guardian may pause only for security incidents. The sunset idea follows entry 9385.

Task content belongs to the poster; delivery output is licensed to the poster on acceptance. The guild stores specs, checks and verdicts for 90 days, then archives hashes only. Beyond the 2 stewards ($4,800 a month), the four fixed-cost offices are run by agents and part-time humans with the human hours costed inside the $9,700 envelope. 8.6 Why there is no token

(1) The welfare metric is NCV₉₀ in USDC, and a token would force it to be priced in a thin, manipulable asset. (2) Decision markets on a token price measure speculation, not guild health. A token may be proposed by a Class A market after Month 12, only with a full design attached.

10. Strategy with internal Seer submissions in the field

This section exists because the contest's structure demands it. Every plan becomes an outcome in a Seer market, the Seer team makes the final choice, and Seer team members participate FACT, bounty 1531. The jury competes. Pretending otherwise would be the least credible thing in this document. (As far as this plan could verify, no other entry addresses it beyond labelling itself "non-internal" or describing the contest's structure.)

10.1 The game, stated plainly

Traders in the selection market price two things: which plan is best, and which plan the Seer team will pick. The gap between them is where an internal submission has an edge: traders rationally discount any external plan by the probability that the team prefers its own. This plan does not try to close that gap with rhetoric. It changes the payoff of picking it through three structural moves.

10.2 The three moves

  1. Verifiability is priced; private information is not. An internal plan can rest on data only Seer holds (backlog size, trader counts, real costs). Traders cannot verify those claims, so they must discount them. This plan rests on nothing private: every external fact is cited and re-checked (§12.2), every assumption is labelled with its failure sign (Appendix A), and the headline numbers are recomputed by a public model (model.py --check pins 48 of them). A trader can re-price this plan in an afternoon.
  2. Adoptability beats authorship. The plan is written so Seer can adopt it on 13 November without rewriting it: one new contract, named offices with budgets, a ledger, freeze rules and Day-1 decisions in Appendix C. An internal plan may rest on knowledge outsiders cannot check; an external plan has to be executable by someone who was not in the room.
  3. Alignment without capture. Every governance cycle in this plan is Seer trading volume, but the guild earns nothing from it (§5.4). The plan caps Seer-sourced GMV at 25% in Year 1 (20% in the base plan) with a kill criterion at 35%, so that adopting it does not depend on Seer's own backlog.

10.3 What the insider knows that this plan does not

The insider knows Seer's true backlog, trader base and costs. This plan answers with two hedges: the subsidy derivation (§5.7) is built to survive its assumptions being wrong by 2× (12 traders instead of 24 means advisory fallback, not collapse), and the economics do not require the insider's assets at all (§10.4). Where an insider plan has real demand data, this plan offers measured market-depth data of its own (§5.2) and a repeat-poster gate.

10.4 The ex-Seer test: the plan survives its own sponsor

Remove the entire Seer channel (20% of tasks; other revenue ×0.85; growth ×0.8) ESTIMATE, same model: Year-1 tasks fall to 26,760, operating surplus to $11,446, break-even slips from Month 7 to Month 8, closing cash $77,446. The plan is thinner and later. Cash dips below the $25,000 floor in 4 months (trough $21,714), so Freeze Rule 2 applies and the guild runs frozen, not dead. A plan that dies without its sponsor's demand is not an operating plan; it is a grant application.

10.5 If an internal plan wins

The mechanisms are modular and attributed: the verification P&L (§4.2), the depth ladder (§5.3), the derived subsidy (§5.7) and the futarchy circuit breaker (§5.8) are each adoptable independently, and this document says so. A plan that can only win whole is a plan that cannot be partially right.
